\begin{abstract}
The single-camp Federalist run (86 essays) found a large \emph{consistent
core}: 7 of 10 variables unanimous where spoken, only 3 conflict sites. We
test whether the STE disagreement structure tracks corpus composition by
adding the opposing camp --- 73 \textbf{Anti-Federalist} essays (Brutus,
Cato, Centinel, Federal Farmer, An Old Whig, Philadelphiensis; public
domain) --- for a \textbf{159-document two-camp corpus}. The same chunking
pipeline extracts a canonical 10-variable schema (now carrying both poles
of each question) and one frame per document; the verified \lean{tides}
executable scores all 159 voices with \lean{STE.Consistent} and
\lean{STE.disagreementDegree} (\lean{Ste.FiniteInstance}). The verdict:
the feasibility set is again empty, but where the Federalist corpus had
\textbf{3} conflict sites the two-camp corpus has \textbf{all 10} --- every
variable is contested, and each split runs cleanly along the
Federalist/Anti-Federalist line. The disagreement profile is a function of
the corpus, computed by the proved algorithm, not a fixed property of the
topic.
\end{abstract}

\section{The experiment}

The Federalist companion note found that 86 essays by Publius share a large
consistent core. The sharp test of whether that structure is intrinsic or
compositional is to add the essays arguing the other side. We fetch 73
Anti-Federalist essays from public-domain sources
(\lean{scripts/antifederalist/}) and pool them with the 86 Federalist
essays into one corpus of \textbf{159 documents}, each a single STE voice.
The pipeline (\lean{scripts/corpus/}, now accepting multiple corpus
directories) runs unchanged: pass~1 (Haiku, per chunk) over 262 chunks
proposes 3{,}253 open $(\text{variable},\text{value})$ pairs; pass~2
(Sonnet) unifies them into a canonical \textbf{10-variable} schema whose
domains now include \emph{both} positions on each contested question;
pass~3 (Haiku, per document) emits one frame per essay. The
\lean{frames.json} is scored by the verified executable, which evaluates
the definitions proved in \lean{Ste.FiniteInstance} on the loaded data (no
\lean{native\_decide}).

\section{The verdict: the core shatters}

\lean{STE.Consistent} $=$ \textsf{false}: no single reading satisfies all
159 voices. But the disagreement is now \emph{total}. Every one of the 10
variables is a conflict site (disagreement degree $\ge 2$), against 3 in the
Federalist-only run:

\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}l c c@{}}
\toprule
variable & Federalist-only & two-camp \\
\midrule
ratification\_stance          & 1 & \textbf{3} \\
governance\_structure         & 3 & 3 \\
standing\_army\_peacetime     & 1 & \textbf{2} \\
bill\_of\_rights\_necessity   & 1 & \textbf{2} \\
federal\_taxation\_scope      & --- & \textbf{2} \\
executive\_power\_character   & 1 & \textbf{2} \\
judiciary\_power\_scope       & 1 & \textbf{2} \\
house\_representation\_adequacy & --- & \textbf{2} \\
confederation\_adequacy       & 1 & \textbf{2} \\
senate\_character             & 2 & \textbf{2} \\
\midrule
conflict sites                & \textbf{3} & \textbf{10} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\noindent (The two schema runs name variables slightly differently; the
invariant claim is the count of conflict sites, $3 \to 10$.)

\section{The split is clean and camp-driven}

The degrees are not extraction noise: spot-checks against the frames show
each conflict is the Federalist/Anti-Federalist divide.

\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}l l l@{}}
\toprule
variable & Federalist essays & Anti-Federalist essays \\
\midrule
ratification\_stance & \vv{favor} (78) & \vv{oppose} (55), \vv{amend} (12) \\
bill\_of\_rights\_necessity & \vv{unnecessary} (2) & \vv{necessary} (25) \\
standing\_army\_peacetime & \vv{safe} (10), \vv{dangerous} (2) & \vv{dangerous} (27), \vv{safe} (1) \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\noindent The degree-3 \vv{ratification\_stance} split is exactly the two
camps plus the conditional-ratification wing (\vv{favor\_with\_amendments}).
The \vv{bill\_of\_rights} axis is cleanly opposite (Federalist~84 vs. the
Anti-Federalist demand). The \vv{standing\_army} split is near-perfect, the
few crossovers reflecting genuine nuance in the sources.

\section{Three shapes, one algorithm}

The same verified \lean{Consistent} / \lean{disagreementDegree} now records
three qualitatively different disagreement shapes:

\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}l l l@{}}
\toprule
corpus & consistent? & structure \\
\midrule
Tides (7) & no & pervasive, multi-camp; worst degree 5 \\
Federalist (86) & no & large consistent core; 3 conflict sites \\
Ratification (159) & no & total shatter; all 10 variables contested \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Adding the opposing 73 essays flips seven unanimous variables into
conflicts. This is precisely the behavior of the modal STE theory: acquiring
information shrinks the feasible family; here it empties the agreement on
\emph{every} axis at once. The disagreement profile is compositional and
computed, not a story told about the topic.

\section{The boundary of trust}

The verdict is the verified STE definitions evaluated on runtime-parsed
frames; each asserted value in \lean{frames.json} carries a licensing quote,
so every cell is auditable against the essay, and the three headline splits
were checked against those quotes. A mis-extraction would move a count, but
the shatter --- ten contested variables where the single camp had three ---
is far larger than any plausible extraction slack.
